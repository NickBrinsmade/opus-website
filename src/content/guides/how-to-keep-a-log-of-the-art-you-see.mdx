<span class="eyebrow">Quick answer</span>

**For each work that stays with you, record four things:** what it is (photograph the label), where you saw it, **one sentence on why it mattered**, and a **rating**. Do it the same day. A notebook is best for thinking, photos are fastest, and an app is easiest to search and share. Whichever you choose, the sentence matters more than the format.
</div>

## Why bother?

Because memory is worse than we think, and cameras make it worse. In 2014 the psychologist **Linda Henkel** took students on a tour of a university art museum, asking them to look at some objects and photograph others. The next day, they remembered fewer of the objects they had photographed, and fewer details about them, than the ones they had just looked at. She called it the **photo-taking impairment effect**: when you trust the camera to remember, you don’t.

There was one exception. When people zoomed in to photograph a specific **detail**, the effect disappeared. Paying attention to a particular part of the work was enough to fix it in memory.

That is the whole case for a log. Writing even one sentence about a painting forces the same kind of attention as zooming in on a detail, and it gives you something to come back to.

## What to record

Keep it short, or you’ll stop doing it. For each work that stays with you:

1. **What it is:** artist, title, date. Photograph the label and you won’t have to write any of this down at the gallery.
2. **Where and when:** the museum, and the exhibition if it was a temporary show. Works travel and rehang, so “I saw it at the Met in 2026” is useful information.
3. **One sentence on why.** Not a review, just what struck you: “The light on the wall behind her is brighter than the window.” “I didn’t expect it to be this small.” “I liked it less than the reproduction.”
4. **A rating.** More on that below.

For a whole exhibition, add a line on the show itself: Was it well chosen? Did it change your mind about the artist? Would you tell a friend to go?

## A rating scale that works

Ratings are useful because they force a decision, and they make your log searchable later (“what were the best things I saw last year?”). The trick is to define your scale once and stick to it. A five-point scale is enough:

- **★★★★★** I’d travel to see it again.
- **★★★★** I’ll remember it. I’d recommend it.
- **★★★** Glad I saw it.
- **★★** Didn’t do much for me.
- **★** I don’t see what others see in it, and I can say why.

Two rules make ratings honest. **Rate what you saw, not the reputation**: a famous painting you found flat in person deserves a low score and a sentence saying why. And **always pair a rating with a sentence**, because three months later the number alone won’t mean anything.

Rating exhibitions is different from rating works. A show of minor works can be a great exhibition if it’s well chosen and well hung, and a show of masterpieces can be a poor one. Rate the show for what it did.

## Notebook, photos or app?

| | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **A notebook or sketchbook** | Thinking. Writing or sketching by hand makes you look harder than anything else. | Hard to search, easy to lose, and you’ll need the label photos anyway. |
| **Your camera roll** | Speed. A photo of the work plus a photo of the label takes ten seconds. | Hundreds of photos you never look at, and no record of what you thought. Add a caption or a favorite tag the same day. |
| **A notes app or spreadsheet** | Searching and sorting. One row per work, with columns for artist, museum, date and rating. | Tedious to keep up on the move. Best filled in on the train home. |
| **A museum or art app** | Doing it all in one place: the work’s details, your rating, your note, and a feed of what friends saw. | Check you can export or keep your data, and that the app lets you write, not just save. |

A combination often works best: label photos in the gallery, then a few minutes on the way home writing the sentence and the rating while it’s fresh.

## Make it a habit

- **Same day, every time.** Memory fades fast. Ten minutes on the train home beats an hour next weekend.
- **Fewer, better entries.** Log the five works that stayed with you, not the fifty you walked past.
- **Look back.** Once a year, read your log and pull out your best ten. You’ll learn what you actually like, which is not always what you think you like.
- **Share some of it.** A friend’s two-line review is the best recommendation there is, and writing for someone else makes your own notes better.
