<div class="answer">
<span class="eyebrow">Quick answer</span>

**The Louvre** has five, including the *Mona Lisa*. **Florence**’s Uffizi has three, and **Milan** has *The Last Supper* (book months ahead), plus the Ambrosiana’s *Portrait of a Musician*. The rest are one or two per city: *Lady with an Ermine* in **Kraków**, *Ginevra de’ Benci* in **Washington** (the only one in the Americas), and others in **London**, **Munich**, **St Petersburg**, the **Vatican** and **Parma**.

## Seeing the Last Supper

*The Last Supper* is a wall painting in the former dining hall of the Dominican monastery of Santa Maria delle Grazie in Milan, and it isn’t going anywhere. Leonardo painted it in an experimental technique on dry plaster, and it began to deteriorate within his lifetime. What you see today is the result of a 20-year restoration finished in 1999.

To protect it, visits are short and tightly controlled:

- **15 minutes**, with at most 40 people in the room. Arrive 30 minutes before your slot, or you lose the ticket.
- **Tickets go on sale in batches** on the official Cenacolo Vinciano site, each covering the next three or four months. The museum announces each release a few days ahead, usually for noon Milan time, and popular dates sell out within hours. Its published schedule says February to April opens in December; as of November 18, 2026, no date had been announced for the first 2027 release.
- **Limits:** tickets carry the visitor’s name, you can buy at most five per calendar year, and they can’t be changed or refunded. A small number of extra tickets goes on sale every Wednesday at noon for the following week.
- **Resellers** charge more. If you miss the release, a guided tour that includes the visit is the realistic fallback.

Combine it with the **Ambrosiana** (the *Portrait of a Musician* and the *Codex Atlanticus*, the largest collection of his notebook pages) and the **Castello Sforzesco**, where he painted the trees on the ceiling of the Sala delle Asse.

## Leonardo’s four women

Only four portraits of women by Leonardo survive, and they’re in four different countries. All four are on this list:

- *Ginevra de’ Benci* (Washington), painted in his early twenties.
- *Lady with an Ermine* (Kraków), Cecilia Gallerani with an ermine in her arms.
- *La Belle Ferronnière* (Paris), currently not on display.
- The *Mona Lisa* (Paris), which he kept working on until close to his death.

Seeing all four is a reasonable lifetime goal.

## Beyond the paintings

Leonardo’s drawings and notebooks are far more numerous than his paintings, but because they’re light-sensitive they’re shown only occasionally.

- **The Royal Collection** in the UK holds around 550 of his drawings, shown in rotating exhibitions.
- **The Vitruvian Man** belongs to the Gallerie dell’Accademia in Venice and is displayed only rarely.
- **The Codex Leicester**, one of his notebooks, is owned by Bill Gates and lent to exhibitions now and then.
- **The Clos Lucé** in Amboise, France, is the manor where he spent his last three years and died in 1519. His tomb is in the chapel of the nearby Château d’Amboise.

## The Mona Lisa, practically

The *Mona Lisa* hangs behind glass in the Louvre’s Salle des États, usually in front of a crowd. It’s small (77 × 53 cm), and you’ll see it from a few meters away. Go at opening or on a late-opening evening, and don’t miss the three other Leonardos on display in the Grande Galerie next door, which almost nobody queues for. It has been stolen once: in 1911 a former Louvre worker walked out with it under his smock, and it was missing for two years.
