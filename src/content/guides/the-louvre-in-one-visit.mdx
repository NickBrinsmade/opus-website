<span class="eyebrow">Quick answer</span>

**The big three in under an hour:** the **Winged Victory** on the Daru staircase, the **Mona Lisa** in the Salle des États, and the **Venus de Milo** in the Sully wing. **Then go where the crowds don’t:** Michelangelo’s *Slaves*, the French grand paintings (*The Raft of the Medusa*, *Liberty Leading the People*), Egypt’s **Seated Scribe**, the **Code of Hammurabi**, and the Napoleon III Apartments. **Book a timed ticket** (€32 for most non-European visitors since January 2026), and go at opening or on a Wednesday or Friday evening.
</div>

**How this list works.** We picked these works ourselves: the ones most people come for, and the ones we think deserve more of a crowd. Room numbers come from the Louvre’s own collection database, checked November 20, 2026. The Louvre rehangs often and is in the middle of a major renovation program, so follow the signs and ask staff if a room is closed.

## Before you start: the layout

The Louvre has three wings, all reached from the hall under the glass pyramid:

- **Denon** (south, along the Seine): Italian and Spanish paintings, large French paintings, Italian sculpture, and the *Mona Lisa*. The busiest wing by far.
- **Sully** (east, around the square courtyard): Greek and Egyptian antiquities, the *Venus de Milo*, and the foundations of the medieval fortress.
- **Richelieu** (north, along the Rue de Rivoli): Near Eastern antiquities, northern European paintings, French sculpture courts and the Napoleon III Apartments. The quietest wing.

## The route

### Denon: the famous ones, fast

1. **The Winged Victory of Samothrace**, at the top of the Daru staircase (Room 703). A Hellenistic goddess of victory landing on the prow of a ship, around 190 BC, placed perfectly at the head of the stairs.
2. **The Mona Lisa**, in the Salle des États (Room 711). Expect a queue, a barrier and a view from a few meters away. It’s small (77 × 53 cm). Take your look, then turn around: the largest painting in the Louvre, Veronese’s *The Wedding Feast at Cana*, covers the opposite wall, and almost nobody looks at it.
3. **The Grande Galerie** (Rooms 710–712), next door. Italian Renaissance painting, including three more **Leonardos** (*Virgin of the Rocks*, *The Virgin and Child with Saint Anne* and *Saint John the Baptist*; his *La Belle Ferronnière* is currently not on display), Raphael and Caravaggio’s *Death of the Virgin* (Room 712). The crowd around the *Mona Lisa* doesn’t follow you here. ([Where to see every Leonardo.](/guides/where-to-see-leonardo-da-vinci-paintings/))
4. **The great French paintings** (Rooms 700–702). *The Raft of the Medusa*, *Liberty Leading the People*, David’s *Coronation of Napoleon* and Ingres’s *Grande Odalisque*. Huge canvases, and the best room in the museum for understanding what French painting was trying to do around 1800–1830.
5. **Michelangelo’s Slaves** (Room 403, the Michelangelo Gallery, ground floor). The *Dying Slave* and *Rebellious Slave*, made for the tomb of Pope Julius II, with Canova’s *Psyche Revived by Cupid’s Kiss* in the same gallery.

### Sully: Greece, Egypt and the medieval Louvre

6. **The Venus de Milo** (Room 345). Found on the island of Milos in 1820. Walk all the way around her.
7. **The medieval Louvre** (lower ground floor). The moat and foundations of Philippe Auguste’s 12th-century fortress, excavated in the 1980s. The museum literally stands on top of its own history.
8. **The Great Sphinx of Tanis** (Room 338), in its own crypt at the entrance to the Egyptian galleries.
9. **The Seated Scribe** (Room 635), upstairs in the Egyptian galleries. About 4,500 years old, with inlaid crystal eyes that seem to follow you.

### Richelieu: the quiet wing

10. **The Code of Hammurabi** (Room 227) and the **Lamassu** (Room 229). The Babylonian law code carved on a basalt stele around 1750 BC, and the giant winged bulls with human heads from the palace of Sargon II at Khorsabad.
11. **The Vermeers.** *The Lacemaker* and *The Astronomer* hang in the Dutch galleries on the second floor (Room 837), often with nobody else in the room. ([Where to see every Vermeer.](/guides/where-to-see-vermeer-paintings/))
12. **Rubens’ Medici cycle.** Twenty-four enormous canvases telling the life of Marie de’ Medici, most of them in the Galerie Médicis (Room 801), built to hold them.
13. **The Napoleon III Apartments.** Red velvet, gold leaf and chandeliers: the state rooms of the Second Empire, preserved as they were. Pure spectacle, and a great place to finish.

## Tickets, entrances and timing

- **Tickets:** book a timed ticket online. Since January 14, 2026, most visitors from outside the EU, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway pay **€32** (€22 for EEA visitors). Under-18s get in free, and so does everyone on the first Friday evening of the month after 6 p.m. (except July and August). From December 19, 2026 to January 4, 2027, a timed reservation is required for everyone.
- **Entrances:** the **pyramid** is the main one and the busiest. The **Carrousel** entrance, through the underground shopping mall from the Rue de Rivoli or the metro, usually has a shorter security line.
- **Closed on Tuesdays.** Open until 9 p.m. on **Wednesday and Friday evenings**, the calmest time to visit. Last entry is an hour before closing, and some rooms may close early, so check the website if there’s one you can’t miss.
- **Photos** are allowed without flash. ([Photo rules at other museums.](/guides/can-you-take-photos-in-museums/))

## What’s changing

In January 2025 France announced a long-term renovation plan for the Louvre, including a new entrance and a dedicated room for the *Mona Lisa*, due around 2031. In October 2025, thieves stole pieces of the French crown jewels from the Galerie d’Apollon in a robbery that took only minutes, and the museum has since been reviewing its security. Expect some rooms to be closed for work on any given day, and check the Louvre’s website for closures before you go.
