<span class="eyebrow">Quick answer</span>

**New York has the most:** eight, at the **Met** (five, all in Gallery 614) and the **Frick** (three). **The Netherlands** has seven within an hour of each other: four at the **Rijksmuseum** in Amsterdam and three, including *Girl with a Pearl Earring*, at the **Mauritshuis** in The Hague. The rest are spread across Washington, London, Paris, Germany, Vienna, Edinburgh, Dublin and Tokyo. One is stolen and one is private.
</div>

**How this list works.** We started from every painting attributed to Vermeer in Wikidata, cross-checked each one against our own museum data, and checked the museums’ sites by hand. That raw list had 44 “Vermeers”. We removed the later copies and the rejected attributions, the collections that no longer exist, and two forgeries (more on those below). What’s left is the 37 paintings scholars discuss as Vermeer’s, two of them disputed. Checked November 5, 2026.

## Vermeer by the numbers

- **35 broadly accepted paintings, plus 2 disputed.** He worked slowly, perhaps two or three paintings a year, and died at 43.
- **19 collections, 8 countries.** The United States holds the most (14, counting the stolen and the private one), then the Netherlands (7), Germany (6) and the UK (5).
- **34 you can see in a normal visit,** if none are away on loan. The other three: one stolen, one private, and one in Buckingham Palace.
- **None in Delft,** the town where he spent his whole life.

## The best Vermeer days

**New York: eight in an afternoon.** Start at the Met, where all five hang together in Gallery 614. That includes *Young Woman with a Water Pitcher* and *A Maid Asleep*. Then walk 15 minutes down Fifth Avenue to the Frick for three more, hung as Henry Clay Frick collected them. This winter the Frick is also showing new paintings by Kent Monkman that respond to its *Officer and Laughing Girl* ([see what else is on in New York](/guides/best-art-exhibitions-new-york/)).

**Amsterdam and The Hague: seven in a day.** Four at the Rijksmuseum (*The Milkmaid*, *The Little Street*, *Woman in Blue Reading a Letter*, *The Love Letter*), then a train of under an hour to the Mauritshuis for *Girl with a Pearl Earring*, *View of Delft* and *Diana and Her Nymphs*. Delft itself is 15 minutes further on. There are no Vermeers there, but you can stand where he painted *View of Delft*.

**Washington, D.C.: three, for free.** The National Gallery of Art has *Woman Holding a Balance*, *A Lady Writing* and the tiny *Girl with the Red Hat*, which is painted on a panel not much bigger than a postcard. Next to them hangs *Girl with a Flute*. Look at the two hats side by side and judge the 2022 reattribution for yourself.

**London: four, two of them free and one almost hidden.** Two at the National Gallery, then *The Guitar Player* at Kenwood House on Hampstead Heath, a free stately home many visitors to London miss. *The Music Lesson* is in the Royal Collection at Buckingham Palace, so it’s only visible when the State Rooms open.

## The Vermeers you can’t see

**The stolen one.** *The Concert* was one of 13 works taken from the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum in Boston in March 1990, often called the biggest art theft in history. It has never been recovered. The Gardner’s founder left instructions that nothing in her galleries should be changed, so the empty frame still hangs where the painting was.

**The private one.** *Young Woman Seated at a Virginal* belongs to the Leiden Collection, a private collection in New York. It’s the only Vermeer in private hands, but the collection lends it to exhibitions, so it does appear from time to time.

**The royal one.** *The Music Lesson* has been in the British Royal Collection since George III bought it in 1762. It hangs in the Picture Gallery at Buckingham Palace, which you can visit only during the palace’s seasonal opening.

## The fake Vermeers

Our raw data included two paintings that look nothing like the rest, dated 1931 and 1937. They’re by **Han van Meegeren**, the most famous art forger of the 20th century. In the 1930s and 1940s he painted “early Vermeers”, biblical scenes he claimed were a missing chapter of Vermeer’s career, and sold them to collectors and museums. One of them, *Christ and the Adulteress*, went to Hermann Göring.

After the war, van Meegeren was arrested for selling a Dutch national treasure to the Nazis, a charge that could have carried the death penalty. His defense was that he had painted it himself. To prove it, he painted another “Vermeer” in front of witnesses. He was convicted of forgery instead, and died before serving his sentence.

It’s a good reminder of why we check databases by hand. Even a well-maintained source can list a forgery under a master’s name.

## Seeing more than one at a time

Exhibitions sometimes bring Vermeers together. In 2023 the Rijksmuseum gathered 28 of them for the largest Vermeer exhibition ever held, and every ticket sold. Shows on that scale are rare, but smaller loans happen every year, so a museum’s exhibition page is worth checking if you’re building a trip around him.

For the rest of the year, the list above is where they hang. If you see one, you’re looking at one of the rarest things in Western art: a painter whose whole surviving work would fit in a single large gallery.
