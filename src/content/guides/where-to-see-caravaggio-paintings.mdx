## Caravaggio in 60 seconds

Michelangelo Merisi, called Caravaggio after his family’s town, arrived in Rome in the early 1590s and within a decade was its most famous painter. He painted saints as ordinary people, with dirty feet, under hard, theatrical light. That made him admired and controversial in equal measure.

He was also violent. In 1606 he killed a man in a brawl and fled Rome with a death sentence on his head. The rest of his life was a run south: **Naples**, then **Malta**, where he was made a knight, then imprisoned, then escaped. Then **Sicily**, Naples again, and death in 1610 on his way back towards Rome to seek a pardon. He was 38.

That route is the map of where his paintings are. Most of them stayed where he left them.

## The best Caravaggio days

**Rome, free: six paintings in a morning.** Start at **San Luigi dei Francesi**, near Piazza Navona, for the three Saint Matthew paintings in the Contarelli Chapel. They were his first public commission and made his name. Walk five minutes to **Sant’Agostino** for the *Madonna di Loreto*, whose kneeling pilgrim shows the dirty soles of his feet, which scandalized people at the time. Then walk 15 minutes north to **Santa Maria del Popolo** for *The Conversion of Saint Paul* and *The Crucifixion of Saint Peter*, side by side in the Cerasi Chapel. All three are working churches: dress modestly, avoid Mass times, and expect some to close in the early afternoon.

**Rome, paid: the Borghese.** Six Caravaggios in one museum is a rare sight, from the early *Boy with a Basket of Fruit* to the late *David with the Head of Goliath*. Entry is in timed slots that sell out, so book well ahead. Add Palazzo Barberini (*Judith Beheading Holofernes*, *Narcissus*), the Doria Pamphilj and the Capitoline Museums for a second day.

**Naples: one great altarpiece.** *The Seven Works of Mercy* still hangs over the altar of the Pio Monte della Misericordia, the charity that commissioned it in 1607. On Via Toledo, the Gallerie d’Italia has *The Martyrdom of Saint Ursula*, probably the last thing he painted.

**Malta: the masterpiece.** *The Beheading of Saint John the Baptist* in St John’s Co-Cathedral in Valletta is more than five meters wide. He signed it in the blood flowing from the saint’s neck, the only signature on any of his paintings.

## The Caravaggios you can’t see

**Stolen.** In October 1969, thieves cut the *Nativity with Saint Francis and Saint Lawrence* from its frame above the altar of the Oratory of San Lorenzo in Palermo. It has never been found, and is often linked to the Sicilian Mafia. A high-resolution reproduction now hangs in its place.

**Destroyed.** Three Caravaggios in Berlin’s museums were lost in May 1945, when fire swept through a flak tower where they had been stored for safety: the first *Saint Matthew and the Angel*, *Portrait of a Courtesan* and *Christ on the Mount of Olives*. They survive only in photographs. Some databases still list them as being in Berlin, one of the reasons we check every entry by hand.

**Locked away.** His only known ceiling painting, *Jupiter, Neptune and Pluto*, is in the Casino dell’Aurora of the Villa Ludovisi in Rome, a private villa that is very rarely open.

## Before you go

- **Churches aren’t museums.** Opening hours change for services and feast days, and lighting can be dim. Check hours on the day and go in the morning.
- **Book the Borghese.** It’s the one Caravaggio visit in Rome you can’t do on a whim.
- **Expect disputed attributions.** Caravaggio was copied heavily in his lifetime, and “new Caravaggios” surface every few years. If a label says “attributed to” or “follower of”, that’s why.
