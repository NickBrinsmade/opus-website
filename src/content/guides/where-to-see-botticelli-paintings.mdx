<span class="eyebrow">Quick answer</span>

**Florence’s Uffizi** has *The Birth of Venus*, *Primavera* and the largest Botticelli collection anywhere. While you’re in Florence, his grave and a fresco are free at the **Church of Ognissanti**. **In Rome,** look at the **Sistine Chapel**’s side walls, not just the ceiling: three of the frescoes are his. **Outside Italy,** London’s National Gallery (free) has *Venus and Mars* and the *Mystic Nativity*.

## A Botticelli day in Florence

1. **The Uffizi, at opening.** Head for the Botticelli rooms first, before the tour groups. *Primavera* and *The Birth of Venus* hang near each other, and both are larger than most people expect. Then find the *Adoration of the Magi*, painted for a Florentine banker, with the Medici family as the kings. Botticelli put himself in the crowd at the far right, in a yellow cloak, staring straight out at the viewer.
2. **Ognissanti, after lunch.** A 15-minute walk west along the river, this church has Botticelli’s fresco of *Saint Augustine in His Study* and his grave, marked by a simple round stone in the floor. Entry is free.
3. **Palazzo Pitti or the Bargello, if you have time.** The Pitti’s Palatine Gallery has a portrait attributed to him. The Bargello has the sculpture of the same Florence he painted.

## Botticelli in the Sistine Chapel

In 1481 Pope Sixtus IV called the best painters in Florence and Umbria to Rome to decorate the walls of his new chapel: Botticelli, Ghirlandaio, Perugino, Rosselli and others. Botticelli painted three of the large scenes, in the stories of Moses and Christ that run along the side walls. Michelangelo’s ceiling followed in 1508–12 and has drawn every eye upward ever since. Next time, look at the walls.

## The forgotten painter

It’s hard to believe now, but after his death in 1510 Botticelli was largely forgotten for more than three centuries. Taste moved on to Leonardo, Raphael and Michelangelo, and his slender, linear figures looked old-fashioned. He was rediscovered in the 19th century by English critics and painters, especially the Pre-Raphaelites. Today *The Birth of Venus* is one of the most reproduced images in the world.

Two stories you’ll hear in Florence, both unproven:

- **That he burned his own paintings** in Savonarola’s “bonfire of the vanities” in 1497. Vasari suggests he fell under Savonarola’s influence, but there’s no record of him burning anything.
- **That Venus is Simonetta Vespucci,** a famous Florentine beauty who died young. It’s a romantic idea, and possible, but nothing in the documents confirms it.

## Before you go

- **Book the Uffizi.** Timed tickets avoid long queues, especially from April to October.
- **Don’t skip the smaller works.** The *Madonna of the Magnificat* and the *Madonna of the Pomegranate*, round paintings called tondi, are in the same rooms and are among his most beautiful.
- **Madrid has a surprise.** The Prado’s three *Nastagio degli Onesti* panels, a gruesome story from Boccaccio painted as a wedding gift, are some of the strangest pictures of the Renaissance.
